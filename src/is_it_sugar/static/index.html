        Measurement

        Only measured amounts count. A label's total sugars cap fructose and sucrose; without them, a sugar with no amount means Avoid. Sorbitol isn't in total sugars, so it always needs a measured amount.

        How it works

        1. Finding the food. Names and barcodes are looked up in USDA and Open Food Facts. An AI model reads photos.
        2. Ingredients. The Boston University HFI table rates sugars. An AI model matches other ingredients to measured foods, or judges them when nothing fits.
        3. Whole foods and dishes. Plain foods use measured sugars from the Australian, French and USDA tables. Dishes use USDA's typical recipe.
        4. How much. Labels list ingredients by weight, so the 3rd is at most a third of the product. The label's total sugars cap the rest.
        5. The verdict. Your Small amounts setting judges the fructose load in a serving. A sugar with no measured amount means Avoid.
        Data sources and research

        Sugar ratings

        • Boston University HFI sugar table: whether each sugar and sweetener is tolerated, except where the research below disagrees.
        • Maltitol, mannitol and isomalt are not tolerated, stevia needs caution and purified steviol glycosides are tolerated, following the Italian survey below. Items rated this way are marked “Research”.

        Measured sugars

        • Australian Food Composition Database, Release 3 (FSANZ, CC BY 4.0): fructose, sucrose and sorbitol in about 900 foods.
        • ANSES-CIQUAL French food composition table, 2020 (Licence Ouverte): fructose and sucrose in about 2,100 foods.
        • USDA FoodData Central: lab-tested (Foundation) and reference (SR Legacy) foods for fructose and sucrose, typical recipes (Survey, FNDDS) for the weight of each ingredient in a dish, and manufacturers' labels (Branded) for ingredient lists, serving sizes and total sugars.

        Products

        • Open Food Facts (ODbL): ingredient lists by barcode, and the other names its ingredient taxonomy gives each food.

        Research

        • Izquierdo-García E, et al. Dietary patterns in a nationwide cohort of patients with hereditary fructose intolerance. Nutrients 2026;18(5):771. Fructose load as fructose + half of sucrose + sorbitol; vegetables grouped under 0.5, 0.5 to 1, and over 1 g per 100 g; and a daily limit of 40 mg per kg of body weight, at most 1 to 1.5 g.
        • Bruni G, et al. Current dietary practices in hereditary fructose intolerance: results from a national survey across 15 Italian metabolic centres. Nutrients 2026;18(16):2609. Of 14 centres, 5 allow vegetables under 0.5 g fructose per 100 g and 9 under 1 g. All 15 prohibit sorbitol and maltitol, and 14 mannitol and isomalt; polydextrose is barely absorbed, and steviol glycosides don't involve fructose.
        • JECFA. Polydextrose specification (1998). Made from about 90 parts glucose and 10 parts sorbitol, mostly bound into the polymer; free glucose and sorbitol at most 6% combined.
        • Health Canada. Isomaltulose (Palatinose): digested into glucose and fructose.
        • The default tiers apply the 0.5 g and 1 g cut-offs to a serving rather than 100 g: under 0.5 g OK, up to 1 g Caution, the low end of the daily limit. No paper gives per-serving tiers.

        AI judgment

        • An AI model picks which measured food an ingredient is, and judges ingredients with no measured data. It can make mistakes.
